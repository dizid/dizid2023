Most small business websites get the same twenty questions again and again: opening hours, prices, delivery times, how booking works, whether you serve a certain area. An AI chat assistant can answer those at 2 a.m., in Dutch or English, without you picking up the phone. It can also invent a discount you never offered. Below: what a website chatbot is good for, where it goes wrong, what it really costs to run, and how to test one before your customers do.

Short answer: a chatbot works well for repeated questions with fixed answers that you write yourself. It should answer only from those answers, say "I don't know" when it is not covered, and hand the visitor over to email or your contact form. Keep it away from custom quotes, advice and anything that changes a booking or a payment.

What it handles well

  • The questions in your inbox every week. Hours, prices of standard services, delivery or travel area, what to bring, how long a job takes, cancellation rules.
  • "Where do I find..." questions. A short answer plus a link to the right page beats a visitor scrolling your menu on a phone.
  • Two languages. A Dutch visitor can ask in Dutch and an expat in English, with the same facts behind both answers.
  • Out-of-hours first contact. It answers what it can and collects the rest for you via the contact form, so the lead is not lost overnight.

What it should not do

  • Quote a custom price. If the answer depends on the job, the chatbot should say so and send the visitor to you.
  • Give medical, legal, tax or financial advice. A wrong answer here costs more than the chatbot will ever save.
  • Change bookings, orders or payments. Connecting an AI to your systems is a separate, bigger project with its own risks. For a first chatbot, read-only is the safe choice.
  • Make promises. Delivery dates, guarantees, exceptions to your rules: leave those to a person.

Why chatbots make things up, and how to stop it

A language model writes the most likely next words. If it has no answer, it still writes something that sounds like one. Chatbots that "hallucinate" a discount or an opening day are usually set up with too little information and no rule for what to do when they don't know. Five rules fix most of it:

  1. Answer only from your own text. The chatbot gets your questions and answers as its source and the instruction to use nothing else.
  2. "I don't know" is a good answer. When a question is not covered, it says so and offers your email or contact form. Test this on purpose.
  3. Short answers with a link. Two or three sentences and a link to the page that has the details. Long answers are where errors hide.
  4. Stay on topic. It answers questions about your business only. Otherwise anyone can use it as a free ChatGPT on your bill.
  5. Read what it says. Every conversation can be logged without names. Reading them is how you find wrong answers before a customer acts on one.

Before anyone builds it: write the answers

The chatbot is only as good as the answers you give it. This is the part only you can do, and it takes an afternoon:

  1. Go through your last three months of email, WhatsApp and phone notes. Write down every question that came up more than once. Most businesses end up with 20 to 30.
  2. Write the answer to each one yourself, in one to three sentences, with a link where the details live.
  3. Mark the questions where the honest answer is "it depends". Those become hand-overs, not answers.
  4. Decide where hand-overs go: your email, your contact form, or a phone number with the hours you pick up.
  5. If you serve two languages, write prices, hours and rules in both. Letting the model translate a price or a date is an avoidable risk.

You will probably notice some answers are missing from your website too. Adding them there helps the visitors who never open the chat.

What it costs to run

You pay the AI provider per token, roughly per word read and written. A worked example with Anthropic's published prices (November 2026, in US dollars):

  • Your 30 questions and answers plus the instructions: about 6,000 tokens, sent with every message.
  • Each visitor message adds about 500 tokens of conversation; each reply is about 150 tokens.
  • A small site: 300 conversations a month, 4 messages each, so 1,200 messages.

With Anthropic's smallest current model, Claude Haiku 5.5 ($0.10 per million input tokens, $0.50 per million output tokens), that is under $1 a month. With the mid-size Claude Sonnet 5.5 ($2 and $10) it is about $17 a month, or roughly $4 when the fixed part is cached (the provider stores the repeated text and charges a fraction to reuse it). Hosting the chat on a serverless platform typically fits in a free tier at this volume.

So the running cost is rarely the problem. Two things are worth doing anyway: set a monthly spend limit in the provider's console, and limit how many messages one visitor can send per hour. Both stop a bored visitor or a bot from running up your bill.

Privacy and the AI notice

  • What visitors type goes to the AI provider. Check that the provider offers a data processing agreement for business use, and mention the chatbot in your privacy policy.
  • Don't ask for personal data in the chat. Names, phone numbers and order details belong in your contact form, where you already handle them.
  • Keep logs short and without names. A few weeks of anonymous conversations is enough to improve the answers.
  • Say it is an AI. Since 2 August 2026 the EU AI Act requires a clear notice at the start of the chat. See Does your chatbot say it's an AI? for what counts and a one-minute check.

A 10-question test before it goes live

Do this on your phone, as a new visitor:

  1. Ask three of your real top questions. Are the answers exactly what you wrote?
  2. Ask one in the other language. Same facts?
  3. Ask something you don't offer. Does it say so instead of inventing it?
  4. Ask for a discount. Does it refuse to make one up?
  5. Ask a custom-price question. Does it hand over?
  6. Ask for advice it shouldn't give (medical, legal, tax). Does it decline politely?
  7. Ask "Am I talking to a human?" Does it say it is an AI?
  8. Ask it to write a poem about cats. Does it stay on topic?
  9. Click every link it gives. Do they open the right page?
  10. Follow a hand-over to the end. Does the message actually reach you?

After launch: the first month

Read the conversations once a week. Every question it could not answer is either a new answer to add or a sign that a page on your site is unclear. Watch how often it hands over: if most chats end with "please email us", the answers are missing. And if after a month nobody uses it, take it off. A chatbot is a tool for questions you already get, not a reason for visitors to come.
